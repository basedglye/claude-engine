## 1. BLOCKING — `SEGMENT_MIN_HOTEL_TIER` gates the quota, not the mix

`alpha-loop` is green on eight of nine assertions and red on:

> `no business-segment guest arrives before the tier-2 renovation, and at
> least one arrives after`

Measured on the tuned build, by replaying the scenario and bucketing
`guest.arrived` payloads around the `hotel.renovated {to: 2}` event:

```
arrival segments: { leisure: 32, family: 24, business: 29 }
business arrivals BEFORE tier 2: 11      AFTER: 18
```

`arrivalsForDay` correctly returns `business: 0` at tiers 0 and 1 and
correctly takes no RNG draws for it — that half is right. But
`guestSpawnSystem` picks each arriving guest's `segment` independently of
`arrivalsBySegment`, so business guests keep walking through the door of
a motel that, by the demand model, none of them booked. The vision's
sentence — *"tier 0 fills only with the cheap segment; tier 2 unlocks the
premium one"* — is therefore not implemented; only its effect on the
daily headcount is.

The fix is in `guestSpawnSystem`: draw the segment from the segments
eligible at the current `hotel.tier` (ideally weighted by that day's
`arrivalsBySegment`, which is already computed and already on the hotel
component's day rollover path). Keep the draw at generation time and keep
the sorted-key iteration, or the stream moves again.

**Do not weaken the assertion.** It is the only thing in the suite that
proves the premium segment is a reward rather than a label, and it caught
a real gap on its first run — which is the best evidence a gate can offer
for its own worth.

## 3. The constants retune (done by me, one pass, `economy.ts` only)

The CEO's ruling: the motel must be a viable if grim business; tier 0
books family **and** leisure; business stays at tier 2; tune the
multipliers, costs and — if needed — the rate ceilings.

### Final constants

```ts
RENOVATE_COST_MINOR        [0, 80_000, 150_000]      // $800, $1,500
RENOVATE_STAR_REQ          [0, 2, 2]                 // unchanged
SEGMENT_MIN_HOTEL_TIER     family 0, leisure 0, business 2
TIER_DEMAND_MULT_PERMILLE  [1000, 1300, 1600]
MAX_RATE_BY_TIER_MINOR     [6_000, 6_500, 7_500]
```

### What was actually wrong, and it was not only the multiplier

The reported symptom was a tier-0 cash plateau, and `TIER_DEMAND_MULT[0]
= 700` was indeed starving day one. But raising it and opening leisure at
tier 0 was **not** enough, and the reason is worth writing down because
it would have been retuned in circles otherwise:

```
day 1  rev  20000   cash  15500   forecast 6
day 2  rev  46500   cash  57500   forecast 4
       >>> RENOVATED to tier 1
day 3  rev  45100   cash  38100   forecast 0     <-- demand dies here
day 4  rev      0   cash  33600   forecast 0
...    (zero revenue for the rest of the run)
```

Demand collapsed **the day after the first renovation**, not before it.
`arrivalsForDay` prices every segment against the *cheapest room rate on
offer*, and the owner bot chases every rate up to `maxRateMinor` whenever
it has nothing else to do. Renovating raised the ceiling from $60 to
$120, the bot promptly charged $120, and a $50-willingness family and a
$60-willingness leisure guest both fell off the price term's clamp. The
hotel priced itself out of its own market as a direct reward for
renovating.

So the rate ceilings are the real control surface, and the rule that
makes them safe is now written in the file: **each tier's ceiling sits at
or just above the lowest willingness among the segments that book at that
tier.** With `[6_000, 6_500, 7_500]` a rate-maximising bot stays inside
everyone's price tolerance, and the tier reward arrives as *more guests*
(the multiplier) rather than as a higher price that drives them away.

### The tuned arc — 14 days, owner bot, seed `hotel-alpha-loop-1`

```
day 1  rev 20000  cash  15500
day 2  rev 46500  cash  57500
       >>> RENOVATED 0 -> 1  ($800)  on day 3
day 3  rev 31100  cash   4100
day 4  rev 41700  cash  41300
day 5  rev 47200  cash  84000
       >>> HIRED day 6, wage $27.50
day 6  rev 47700  cash 124450
       >>> RENOVATED 1 -> 2  ($1,500) on day 7
day 7  rev 31900  cash   -900        <-- the renovation genuinely hurts
day 8  rev  2500  cash  -5650
day 9  rev 61000  cash  48100
day 10 rev 53200  cash  94050
...
day 14 rev 41400  cash 310850        <-- closes at $3,108.50, solvent
85 arrivals, 76 check-ins, 2 renovations
```

Against the CEO's three criteria: **(a)** tier 2 reached on day 7 of 14,
solvent at the end — met with a week to spare. **(b)** the hire beat
holds: `one-man-week` passes with **every one of its twelve original
assertions unchanged and none weakened**. **(c)** tension is real —
tier 1 takes 3 days (the stated minimum), and the tier-2 renovation puts
the hotel **into overdraft for two days** before the premium segment pays
it back. That two-day hole is the best thing in the trajectory and I
tuned toward keeping it.

I also tried `[100_000, 180_000]`. It pushed the hotel into a
desk-service wedge — 58 arrivals but only 38 queued and 30 presented,
with revenue flatlining for six-day stretches. That is a bot/desk
fragility the constants merely expose, and it is **flagged for cycle 2**:
the loop has an unmodelled throughput ceiling that appears when cash is
tight for several consecutive days.

**Step 2 is a finding, not a pass.** Loosening the sim's cash gate
changes nothing, because the owner bot only presses RENOVATE when
LEDGER's `renovateAvailable` is true, and that view flag independently
checks cash against the same constant. **The sim-side cash refusal is
never exercised by this scenario** — the scenario proves the *view's*
courtesy guard, and the rule behind it is untested from the command path.
That is the anti-cheat posture this project cares about most (the app
proposes, the sim decides), so it should get its own coverage: have the
bot submit a bare `renovateCommand` while broke and assert
`screen.denied { reason: "insufficient-cash" }`.

So I also ran the perturbation in the direction that *does* bite —
making the gate stricter, which is what the brief originally specified:
